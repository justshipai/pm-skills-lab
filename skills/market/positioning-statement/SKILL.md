# Positioning statement

Positioning is the context you set so customers instantly "get" why your product is valuable *and for them*. Most attempts are either a tagline or a feature list. This skill builds positioning the way April Dunford does it: start from the **competitive alternatives**, find the **attributes you uniquely have**, translate those into the **value** customers get, identify **who cares most** about that value, and frame the **market category** that makes it all make sense. Then write the statement.

## The five components (Dunford)

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would customers use if you didn't exist (including "a spreadsheet" or "nothing")? Positioning is relative to these.
2. **Unique attributes** — what you have that the alternatives don't (features, model, data, focus).
3. **Value (so what)** — the benefit those attributes enable, in the customer's terms. Attributes → value.
4. **Who cares most** — the customers for whom that value is most compelling (your best-fit segment). Strong positioning is for *someone*, not everyone.
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference you compete in, chosen so your strengths look obvious. The category sets expectations.

## The judgment this skill encodes

- **Position against the real alternative, including "do nothing."** Value is relative; name what you're better *than*.
- **Attributes are not value.** "Real-time sync" is an attribute; "your team never works off stale data" is the value. Always translate.
- **For someone, not everyone.** Pick the segment that most acutely needs your unique value; trying to position for all dilutes to mush.
- **Category is a choice with consequences.** The category you claim sets the comparison set and the buyer's expectations — choose the one where your strengths are the deciding factors.
- **Differentiated and true.** Positioning must be both unique vs. alternatives and credibly deliverable — not aspirational marketing.
- **Statement last.** Only after the five components is the classic statement ("For [target] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [value], unlike [alternative], because [unique attributes]") worth writing.

## Process

1. **List competitive alternatives** (incl. status quo / DIY / nothing).
2. **Identify unique attributes** vs. those alternatives.
3. **Translate attributes → value** in customer terms.
4. **Name who cares most** — the best-fit segment.
5. **Choose the market category** that frames your strengths as decisive.
6. **Write the positioning statement** from the components.

## Output format

```
# <Product> — Positioning

## Competitive alternatives    (incl. "do nothing")
## Unique attributes           (what only we have)
## Value (so what)             (attributes → customer benefit)
## Who cares most               (best-fit segment & why)
## Market category              (the frame we choose, and why)

## Positioning statement
> For [target customer] who [need/struggle], [product] is a [market category]
> that [key value], unlike [primary alternative], because [unique attributes].
```

## Anti-patterns to refuse

- A tagline or slogan instead of positioning → Work the five components first.
- Listing attributes/features as if they were value → Translate each to the "so what."
- Positioning "for everyone" → Choose the best-fit segment.
- Ignoring the status-quo alternative → Include "do nothing / DIY."
- Picking a category where competitors' strengths dominate → Reframe to where yours do.
