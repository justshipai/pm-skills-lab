# Playing to Win

Strategy is choice, and choice means giving things up. Roger Martin's "Playing to Win" cascade forces the choices most "strategies" dodge: where you'll compete, where you won't, and how you'll actually win there. This skill produces a real strategy cascade — five linked choices — and insists on the trade-offs, because a strategy that has no "we won't" is just a wish list.

## The cascade (five linked choices)

1. **Winning aspiration** — what does winning look like, and for whom? (Purpose + the win condition, not a vanity goal.)
2. **Where to play** — the specific arena: which customers/segments, geographies, channels, product categories. And, explicitly, **where you won't play.**
3. **How to win** — your durable advantage *in that arena*: how you'll win that you can sustain (cost, differentiation, network, etc.). Not "be better" — *why* you'll win.
4. **Capabilities** — the few capabilities you must have/build to win this way.
5. **Management systems** — the systems, metrics, and structures that build and sustain those capabilities.

## The judgment this skill encodes

- **Choices, not goals.** "Grow 30%" is a goal; "win mid-market by being the only tool a non-technical admin can run" is a choice. Cascade = choices.
- **Where-to-play requires a where-NOT-to-play.** If you're "for everyone," you've made no choice. Name who and what you're deliberately giving up.
- **How-to-win must be defensible and arena-specific.** Explain *why* you win where you've chosen to play, and why it's hard to copy. "Better UX" is not a how-to-win unless you say why it persists.
- **The choices must reinforce each other.** Where-to-play and how-to-win have to fit; capabilities must serve the how-to-win; systems must build the capabilities. Flag incoherence.
- **State what must be true.** For the strategy to work, certain things must hold (about customers, competitors, capabilities). Name them — they're your biggest risks and test targets.
- **Resist the everything-strategy.** If the cascade could describe any competitor, it isn't a strategy. Push for the uncomfortable, specific choice.

## Process

1. **Winning aspiration** — the win condition and who it serves.
2. **Where to play** — the arena, specific; plus explicit non-choices.
3. **How to win** — the defensible, arena-specific advantage and why it lasts.
4. **Capabilities** — the few that matter.
5. **Management systems** — what builds/sustains them.
6. **Coherence check + what-must-be-true** — do the choices reinforce each other; what has to hold; biggest risks to test.

## Output format

```
# <Product/Business> — Strategy Cascade (Playing to Win)

## 1. Winning aspiration
## 2. Where to play            (the arena — and explicitly where we won't)
## 3. How to win               (defensible advantage in that arena; why it lasts)
## 4. Capabilities             (the few we must have)
## 5. Management systems        (what builds & sustains them)
## Coherence & what must be true   (do the choices reinforce? what has to hold? key risks)
```

## Anti-patterns to refuse

- Goals/initiatives presented as strategy → Reframe as the five choices.
- "Where to play: everyone" → Force a real segment and an explicit where-NOT-to-play.
- "How to win: be the best / better UX" → Demand a defensible, arena-specific reason you win.
- Choices that don't fit together → Flag the incoherence.
- A cascade any competitor could also write → Push for the specific, costly choice.
